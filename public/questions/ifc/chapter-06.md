# Chapter 6: Tax and Retirement Planning

Original practice questions grounded in the CSI Investment Funds in Canada (IFC) textbook, Chapter 6: taxation of investment income, government and employer pension plans, RRSPs, RRIFs, TFSAs, and RESPs/CESGs.

---

1. A client holds a GIC that pays annual interest. For tax purposes, this interest income is:
    - A. 50% taxable, like a capital gain.
    - B. Fully taxable at the client's marginal rate.
    - C. Eligible for the dividend tax credit.
    - D. Tax-free if the GIC is held to maturity.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: B
      Explanation: Interest income is fully taxable at the investor's marginal rate, with no inclusion rate discount and no credit. That makes it the most heavily taxed form of investment income.
    </details>

2. An investor realizes a $10,000 capital gain on shares held outside a registered account. How much is included in taxable income?
    - A. $10,000.
    - B. $2,500.
    - C. $7,500.
    - D. $5,000.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
      Explanation: Only 50% of a capital gain is included in taxable income: $10,000 × 50% = $5,000.
    </details>

3. Eligible Canadian dividends are "grossed-up" before being included in a taxpayer's income. The purpose of the gross-up is:
    - A. To approximate the pre-tax corporate earnings behind the dividend.
    - B. To ensure dividends are taxed more heavily than interest.
    - C. To convert dividend income into capital gains for tax purposes.
    - D. To reduce the dividend amount that must be reported.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: A
      Explanation: The gross-up approximates the corporation's pre-tax earnings. The dividend tax credit then offsets the tax the corporation already paid, which reduces double taxation.
    </details>

4. Compared to eligible dividends from Canadian corporations, dividends from foreign corporations are:
    - A. Eligible for the Canadian dividend tax credit.
    - B. Taxed like capital gains, at a 50% inclusion rate.
    - C. Taxed like interest, with no dividend tax credit.
    - D. Exempt from Canadian tax if already taxed abroad.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: C
      Explanation: Foreign dividends don't qualify for the dividend tax credit, so they are taxed like interest income, at the full marginal rate.
    </details>

5. An investor has a capital loss on a stock sale in a non-registered account. Can the loss be used to reduce their employment income for the year?
    - A. Yes; capital losses can offset any type of income.
    - B. No; capital losses generally offset only capital gains.
    - C. Yes, but only up to $3,000 per year.
    - D. No; capital losses can never be used for tax purposes.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: B
      Explanation: In Canada, capital losses can generally only be applied against capital gains, not employment, interest, or dividend income. The $3,000 annual offset in option C is a U.S. rule.
    </details>

6. Which statement about Canada Pension Plan (or Quebec Pension Plan) contributions is correct?
    - A. Contributions are voluntary for salaried employees.
    - B. Only self-employed individuals are required to contribute.
    - C. Benefits are forfeited if the contributor leaves Canada.
    - D. Contributions are mandatory and the entitlement is irrevocable.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
      Explanation: Contributions are automatic for anyone with employment or self-employment income, and the pension entitlement they earn is irrevocable — it isn't lost by leaving Canada.
    </details>

7. A client wants to start receiving CPP payments at age 60 instead of 65. What happens to the monthly pension?
    - A. It is permanently reduced.
    - B. It is unchanged.
    - C. It is permanently increased.
    - D. It is reduced only until age 65.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: A
      Explanation: Starting CPP before 65 permanently reduces the monthly amount; it doesn't go back up at 65. Deferring past 65 increases it.
    </details>

8. To qualify for a full Old Age Security pension, an applicant generally must have lived in Canada for at least how many years after turning 18?
    - A. 10 years.
    - B. 20 years.
    - C. 40 years.
    - D. 45 years.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: C
      Explanation: A full OAS pension requires 40 years of residence in Canada after age 18. Ten years is the minimum for a partial pension.
    </details>

9. A high-income retiree's Old Age Security payments are reduced because their net income exceeds a set threshold. This reduction is known as:
    - A. The dividend gross-up.
    - B. A pension adjustment.
    - C. A deemed disposition.
    - D. The OAS clawback.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
      Explanation: The Income Tax Act requires high-income pensioners to repay some or all of their OAS. This is known as the clawback.
    </details>

10. In which type of registered pension plan does the member know, in advance, the formula that will determine their pension at retirement?
    - A. A defined contribution plan.
    - B. A defined benefit plan.
    - C. A pooled registered pension plan.
    - D. A group RRSP.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: B
      Explanation: A defined benefit plan promises a pension set by a formula (for example, a flat amount or a percentage of salary per year of service). In a defined contribution plan, only the contributions are known.
    </details>

11. An employee in a flat benefit pension plan earns $20 per month for each year of service. After 25 years of service, what is the monthly pension?
    - A. $250.
    - B. $20.
    - C. $500.
    - D. $5,000.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: C
      Explanation: Flat benefit = benefit per year of service × years of service = $20 × 25 = $500 per month.
    </details>

12. A key disadvantage of a flat benefit pension plan is that:
    - A. Its real value erodes with inflation unless it is renegotiated.
    - B. It cannot be offered to unionized employees.
    - C. It pays higher benefits to higher earners.
    - D. It cannot be combined with CPP or OAS benefits.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: A
      Explanation: The flat dollar amount doesn't adjust automatically, so inflation erodes its value unless it's renegotiated. It also doesn't vary with earnings, so option C is wrong.
    </details>

13. An employee in a career average pension plan earns 2% of average salary per year of service. Over a 20-year career, their average salary was $50,000. What is the annual pension?
    - A. $50,000.
    - B. $2,000.
    - C. $10,000.
    - D. $20,000.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
      Explanation: Pension = average salary × benefit rate × years of service = $50,000 × 2% × 20 = $20,000 per year.
    </details>

14. A final average pension plan bases benefits on earnings in the final years of service (e.g., the best 5 of the last 10 years) rather than the career average. The main reason is that it:
    - A. Is simpler to administer than a career average plan.
    - B. Better protects the pension against inflation.
    - C. Always produces a smaller pension than a career average plan.
    - D. Removes the need for employer contributions.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: B
      Explanation: Earnings near retirement reflect years of inflation and raises, so basing the pension on them protects its real value better than averaging in low, early-career salaries.
    </details>

15. A key disadvantage of a defined contribution (money purchase) pension plan, compared with a defined benefit plan, is that:
    - A. The employer bears all of the investment risk.
    - B. Contributions can be invested in only one fund.
    - C. The final pension amount isn't known until retirement.
    - D. Employees cannot choose how contributions are invested.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: C
      Explanation: In a DC plan the pension depends on how the invested contributions perform, so the employee bears the investment risk and doesn't know the final amount until retirement.
    </details>

16. Combined employer and employee contributions to a defined contribution plan cannot exceed the lesser of the annual dollar limit and:
    - A. 18% of the employee's current-year compensation.
    - B. 18% of the employee's previous-year earned income.
    - C. 10% of the employee's current-year compensation.
    - D. 50% of the employee's current-year compensation.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: A
      Explanation: DC plans use 18% of current-year compensation. The RRSP limit, by contrast, is based on 18% of the previous year's earned income.
    </details>

17. A client earned $80,000 last year, has $5,000 of unused RRSP room carried forward, and has no pension adjustment. Assuming 18% of income is below the annual dollar limit, what is the maximum RRSP contribution this year?
    - A. $14,400.
    - B. $5,000.
    - C. $27,830.
    - D. $19,400.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
      Explanation: New room is 18% × $80,000 = $14,400. Adding the $5,000 carried forward gives $19,400. The $27,830 dollar limit doesn't apply because 18% of income is lower.
    </details>

18. For an RRSP contribution to be deductible for a given taxation year, it must generally be made:
    - A. During the calendar year only.
    - B. During the year or within 60 days after year-end.
    - C. Within 90 days after year-end.
    - D. Any time before the tax return is filed.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: B
      Explanation: Contributions made during the year or in the first 60 days of the following year can be deducted for that tax year.
    </details>

19. Which TWO of the following correctly describe the RRSP over-contribution rules? (Choose TWO)
    - A. Over-contributions of up to $2,000 can be made without a penalty tax.
    - B. Any over-contribution, even $1, triggers a penalty tax.
    - C. A penalty of 1% per month applies to the excess over $2,000.
    - D. Over-contributions are fully tax-deductible.
    - E. A penalty of 1% per year applies to the entire over-contribution.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: A, C
      Explanation: There is a $2,000 lifetime over-contribution cushion. Anything above it is taxed at 1% per month until it is removed or absorbed by new room.
    </details>

20. A client contributes mutual fund units they already own to their self-directed RRSP instead of cash. For tax purposes, this is treated as:
    - A. A tax-free transfer with no tax consequences.
    - B. A deemed disposition, with any loss fully deductible.
    - C. An addition to RRSP room rather than a contribution.
    - D. A deemed disposition at fair market value.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
      Explanation: A contribution in kind is treated as a sale at fair market value, so any capital gain is taxable. Any capital loss is deemed to be nil, which is why option B is wrong.
    </details>

21. A husband contributes to his wife's spousal RRSP and claims the deduction. If she withdraws the funds in the same year or the next two calendar years, the withdrawal is generally taxed to:
    - A. The annuitant spouse.
    - B. The contributing spouse.
    - C. Both spouses equally.
    - D. Neither spouse.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: B
      Explanation: Under the attribution rule, withdrawals of contributions made in the year of withdrawal or the two previous calendar years are taxed to the contributor. This stops couples from using spousal RRSPs for short-term income splitting.
    </details>

22. By the end of the year in which the holder turns what age must an RRSP be converted to a RRIF, used to buy an annuity, or collapsed?
    - A. 71.
    - B. 65.
    - C. 69.
    - D. 75.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: A
      Explanation: RRSPs must mature by the end of the year the holder turns 71. The options are a RRIF, an annuity, or a taxable lump-sum withdrawal.
    </details>

23. Each year, a RRIF holder is required to withdraw:
    - A. A fixed amount set when the RRIF was opened.
    - B. Nothing; RRIF withdrawals are optional.
    - C. At least the annual minimum, with no maximum.
    - D. At most the annual maximum, with no minimum.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: C
      Explanation: A RRIF requires a minimum withdrawal each year, based on a formula tied to age, but there's no maximum. Every withdrawal is taxable.
    </details>

24. Which of the following correctly describes a TFSA?
    - A. Contributions are deductible, and withdrawals are taxable.
    - B. Contributions are deductible, and withdrawals are tax-free.
    - C. Contributions aren't deductible, and withdrawals are taxable.
    - D. Contributions aren't deductible, and withdrawals are tax-free.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
      Explanation: TFSA contributions are made with after-tax dollars, and both the growth and the withdrawals are tax-free. Option A describes an RRSP.
    </details>

25. A client withdraws $5,000 from their TFSA this year. When is that amount added back to their contribution room?
    - A. Immediately, in the same year.
    - B. At the start of the following year.
    - C. After a three-year waiting period.
    - D. Never; withdrawals permanently use up room.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: B
      Explanation: TFSA withdrawals are added back to contribution room on January 1 of the next year. Re-contributing the money in the same year could cause an over-contribution.
    </details>

26. What is the lifetime maximum contribution permitted per beneficiary under a Registered Education Savings Plan?
    - A. $50,000.
    - B. $36,000.
    - C. $7,200.
    - D. There is no lifetime maximum.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: A
      Explanation: The RESP lifetime contribution limit is $50,000 per beneficiary. $7,200 is the lifetime maximum for the CESG, not for contributions.
    </details>

27. Which TWO of the following correctly describe the Canada Education Savings Grant (CESG)? (Choose TWO)
    - A. It matches 100% of contributions up to $2,500 a year.
    - B. It counts toward the $50,000 lifetime contribution limit.
    - C. The basic grant is 20% of the first $2,500 contributed each year.
    - D. It never has to be repaid under any circumstances.
    - E. The lifetime maximum grant per beneficiary is $7,200.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: C, E
      Explanation: The basic CESG is 20% of the first $2,500 contributed each year (up to $500), with a lifetime maximum of $7,200. Grants don't count toward the $50,000 limit and may have to be repaid if the beneficiary doesn't pursue post-secondary education.
    </details>

28. When a beneficiary withdraws RESP income (not original contributions) to attend a qualifying post-secondary program, that income is taxed:
    - A. To neither the contributor nor the beneficiary.
    - B. In the contributor's hands at their marginal rate.
    - C. In the contributor's hands at a reduced rate.
    - D. In the beneficiary's hands, usually at a low rate.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
      Explanation: Grants and investment income paid out as Educational Assistance Payments are taxed to the student, who usually has little other income and so pays little or no tax.
    </details>
