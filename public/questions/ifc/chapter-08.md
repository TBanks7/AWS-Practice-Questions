# Chapter 8: Constructing Investment Portfolios

Original practice questions grounded in the CSI Investment Funds in Canada (IFC) textbook, Chapter 8: risk and return, inflation and taxation, calculating returns (arithmetic vs. geometric mean, portfolio return), measuring risk (variance, standard deviation, beta, alpha, duration), diversification and correlation, portfolio management styles, strategic asset allocation, and fundamental vs. technical analysis.

---

1. The expected return formula for a single security includes expected cash flow plus expected capital gain (or minus capital loss), divided by:
    - A. The expected ending value.
    - B. The beginning value of the investment.
    - C. The standard deviation.
    - D. The inflation rate.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: B
    </details>

2. A client earned a nominal return of 6% on an investment last year, while inflation was 2.5%. Approximately what real rate of return did the client actually earn?
    - A. 8.5%.
    - B. 2.5%.
    - C. 6%.
    - D. Approximately 3.5%.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
    </details>

3. If a client's nominal return exactly equals the inflation rate for the year, what has happened to their purchasing power?
    - A. It has been maintained, but their real return is 0% and their wealth has not increased.
    - B. It has increased significantly.
    - C. It has been completely wiped out.
    - D. It is impossible to determine without knowing the exact dollar amount.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: A
    </details>

4. Of interest income, dividend income, and capital gains, which is generally taxed most heavily for a Canadian investor?
    - A. Capital gains, since only 50% is included in income.
    - B. Dividend income, due to the dividend tax credit.
    - C. Interest income, which is taxed at the same rate as employment income with no special credit or reduced inclusion.
    - D. All three are always taxed at identical rates.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: C
    </details>

5. An investor purchased a stock for $20, received $1 in dividends during the year, and sold it a year later for $22. What was the rate of return?
    - A. 5%.
    - B. 15%.
    - C. 10%.
    - D. 22%.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: B
    </details>

6. A fund's five-year annual returns were highly volatile, including both large gains and losses. Comparing the arithmetic mean and geometric mean of these returns, the representative should expect that:
    - A. The geometric mean will always be higher than the arithmetic mean.
    - B. Both means will always be identical, regardless of volatility.
    - C. The arithmetic mean cannot be calculated for volatile returns.
    - D. The arithmetic mean will be higher than the geometric mean, since the two are only equal when returns are identical each year.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
    </details>

7. When a client wants to know the actual compound growth rate their money achieved over a five-year holding period, the representative should reference the fund's:
    - A. Geometric mean return, since it reflects the true change in wealth over the period.
    - B. Arithmetic mean return, since it is always more accurate.
    - C. Beta, since it measures compound growth.
    - D. Standard deviation, since it measures average return.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: A
    </details>

8. A client invests $60 in Fund A, expected to return 15%, and $40 in Fund B, expected to return 12%. What is the expected return on the overall portfolio?
    - A. 27%.
    - B. 12%.
    - C. 13.8% [(0.15 x 0.60) + (0.12 x 0.40)].
    - D. 15%.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: C
    </details>

9. Two funds have both averaged a 12% annual return over five years, but Fund X has a standard deviation of 10% while Fund Y has a standard deviation of 4%. Which fund is considered riskier, and why?
    - A. Fund Y, because a lower standard deviation always signals more risk.
    - B. Fund X, because its higher standard deviation implies a wider probable range of future outcomes.
    - C. Neither, since both funds have earned identical average returns.
    - D. It is impossible to compare risk using standard deviation.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: B
    </details>

10. A fund has an average annual return of 12% and a standard deviation of 10%. What is its approximate probable range of returns?
    - A. 0% to 12%.
    - B. 12% to 22% only.
    - C. Exactly 12%, with no variability.
    - D. 2% to 22% (12% - 10% to 12% + 10%).

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
    </details>

11. By convention, the overall stock market is assigned a beta of:
    - A. 1.0.
    - B. 0.
    - C. 100.
    - D. It varies every day and has no fixed value.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>

    </details>

12. An equity fund has a beta of 1.30. If the S&P/TSX Composite Index rises by 10%, the fund would be expected to rise by approximately:
    - A. 10%.
    - B. 1.3%.
    - C. 13%.
    - D. 30%.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>

    </details>

13. Which TWO of the following statements about correlation and diversification are correct? (Choose TWO)
    - A. Combining two securities with a correlation of +1 (perfect positive correlation) maximizes diversification benefit.
    - B. Combining two securities with a correlation of -1 (perfect negative correlation) provides the maximum possible diversification benefit.
    - C. Adding a second gold mining stock to a portfolio already holding one gold mining stock meaningfully diversifies the portfolio.
    - D. Systematic (market) risk cannot be eliminated through diversification, no matter how many securities are added.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: B, D
    </details>

14. The risk that remains in a well-diversified equity portfolio, arising from broad factors such as inflation, the business cycle, and interest rates, is known as:
    - A. Market risk, or systematic risk.
    - B. Firm-specific risk, which diversification eliminates entirely.
    - C. Default risk exclusively.
    - D. Currency risk exclusively.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: A
    </details>

15. A portfolio manager's skill in selecting securities that outperform what would be expected purely from the portfolio's beta is measured by:
    - A. Standard deviation.
    - B. Duration.
    - C. Alpha, the excess return earned on the portfolio.
    - D. The correlation coefficient.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>

    </details>

16. A portfolio manager believes that stock prices already reflect all publicly available information, so it is not possible to consistently identify undervalued securities. This manager believes markets are:
    - A. Highly inefficient.

    </details>

17. A portfolio manager receives material, non-public information about a company from a personal contact and trades on it before the information becomes public. This is:
    - A. A legitimate application of fundamental analysis.

18. A portfolio manager who simply buys and holds a broad stock market index, believing markets are efficient and not attempting to identify mispriced securities, is practicing:

19. The long-term base policy mix of asset classes (e.g., 60% equities and 40% bonds) that a portfolio manager adheres to over time, rebalancing as needed, is called the:

20. According to the textbook, of all the decisions a portfolio manager makes, the one that accounts for most of a portfolio's success or failure is:
    - A. The selection of a single "hot" stock.
