# Chapter 5: Behavioural Finance

Original practice questions grounded in the CSI Investment Funds in Canada (IFC) textbook, Chapter 5: behavioural finance, cognitive vs. emotional biases, gender differences in investing, and building a client's "best practical allocation."

---

1. Behavioural finance is best defined as:
    - A. The study of how interest rates affect financial markets.
    - B. The regulatory framework governing investor behaviour.
    - C. The application of psychology to finance and investing.
    - D. A model that assumes investors always act rationally.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: C
      Explanation: Behavioural finance combines psychology and finance to explain how investors actually behave. Standard finance, by contrast, assumes rational behaviour.
    </details>

2. Standard (traditional) finance theory generally assumes investors are:
    - A. Risk averse and rational.
    - B. Risk seeking and rational.
    - C. Risk averse and emotional.
    - D. Risk neutral and irrational.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: A
      Explanation: Standard finance assumes investors are rational and risk averse. Behavioural finance developed partly because real behaviour often departs from that assumption.
    </details>

3. The tendency for stocks, particularly small stocks, to move abnormally higher during the month of January is known as:
    - A. The endowment effect.
    - B. The disposition effect.
    - C. The recency effect.
    - D. The January Effect.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
      Explanation: The January Effect is a market anomaly that standard finance theory has trouble explaining, and it is one of the observations that gave rise to behavioural finance.
    </details>

4. One client's bias stems from a subconscious information-processing shortcut; another client's stems from an involuntary emotional reaction. These are, respectively:
    - A. Both emotional biases.
    - B. A cognitive bias, then an emotional bias.
    - C. Both cognitive biases.
    - D. An emotional bias, then a cognitive bias.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: B
      Explanation: Cognitive biases come from faulty reasoning or information-processing shortcuts. Emotional biases come from feelings and impulses.
    </details>

5. A client refuses to sell a stock for less than the price they originally paid, even though its fundamentals have deteriorated, because they remain fixated on that purchase price. This illustrates:
    - A. Availability bias.
    - B. Endowment bias.
    - C. Anchoring bias.
    - D. Representativeness bias.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: C
      Explanation: Anchoring is relying too heavily on an initial reference point — here, the purchase price — instead of current information.
    </details>

6. Cognitive biases, unlike emotional biases, can often be corrected through:
    - A. Waiting for the market to recover.
    - B. Adapting the portfolio to the bias.
    - C. Increasing the client's equity exposure.
    - D. Better information and education.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
      Explanation: Cognitive biases come from faulty reasoning, so better information and advice can often correct them. Emotional biases are harder to change and are often adapted to instead.
    </details>

7. An investor receives an unverified stock tip from an acquaintance and immediately invests a large sum, believing they have better insight than other market participants. This illustrates:
    - A. Overconfidence.
    - B. Regret aversion.
    - C. Hindsight bias.
    - D. Status quo bias.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: A
      Explanation: Overconfidence is overestimating your own knowledge or ability — here, believing an unverified tip gives you an edge over the market.
    </details>

8. A client sees that a new fund shares a few surface features with a past fund that performed extremely well, and concludes the new fund must also be excellent. This illustrates:
    - A. Loss aversion.
    - B. Representativeness bias.
    - C. Status quo bias.
    - D. Anchoring bias.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: B
      Explanation: Representativeness is judging something by how closely it resembles a familiar category or example, while ignoring the differences that matter.
    </details>

9. After a stock rises sharply, an investor insists they "knew it all along," even though the outcome was genuinely unpredictable at the time. This is an example of:
    - A. Availability bias.
    - B. Endowment bias.
    - C. Overconfidence.
    - D. Hindsight bias.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
      Explanation: Hindsight bias is seeing past events as having been predictable after the fact. Over time it can feed overconfidence.
    </details>

10. An investor assumes a heavily advertised fund company must be a superior choice, simply because its ads are seen so often. This illustrates:
    - A. Anchoring bias.
    - B. Representativeness bias.
    - C. Availability bias.
    - D. Overconfidence.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: C
      Explanation: Availability bias is judging how likely or how good something is by how easily it comes to mind. Frequent advertising makes a company more familiar, not better.
    </details>

11. A client demands a much higher price to sell a security they own than they would be willing to pay to buy the same security today. This illustrates:
    - A. Endowment bias.
    - B. Status quo bias.
    - C. Loss aversion.
    - D. Anchoring bias.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: A
      Explanation: Endowment bias is valuing something more simply because you own it. Standard economic theory says the price you'd sell at and the price you'd buy at should be the same.
    </details>

12. In the textbook's diagnostic example, a client chooses between an assured gain of $475 and a 25% chance of gaining $2,000 (75% chance of gaining nothing). Which TWO statements are correct? (Choose TWO)
    - A. The assured $475 has the higher expected value.
    - B. The 25% chance at $2,000 has the higher expected value.
    - C. A loss-averse client is likely to choose the probabilistic option.
    - D. A loss-averse client is likely to choose the assured $475.
    - E. Loss aversion has no bearing on how a client answers.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: B, D
      Explanation: The gamble's expected value is 25% × $2,000 = $500, which is more than $475. A loss-averse client still tends to take the sure gain, giving up expected value.
    </details>

13. Industry veterans use the term "get-even-itis" to describe a client who:
    - A. Sells any investment at the first sign of a loss.
    - B. Buys more of a stock every time it rises.
    - C. Holds a losing investment until it gets back to break-even.
    - D. Refuses to invest until markets have fully recovered.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: C
      Explanation: Get-even-itis is a form of loss aversion: the client won't accept a loss and holds a losing position far too long, waiting to break even.
    </details>

14. A client refuses to buy an undervalued stock during a downturn, not because of any analysis, but because they fear whatever they decide will look like a mistake later. This illustrates:
    - A. Regret aversion.
    - B. Overconfidence.
    - C. Loss aversion.
    - D. Status quo bias.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: A
      Explanation: Regret aversion is avoiding decisions out of fear that they will turn out badly. It often leads to inaction or overly conservative choices.
    </details>

15. A client keeps a long-held mutual fund purely out of familiarity and comfort, despite years of underperformance relative to suitable alternatives. This illustrates:
    - A. Availability bias.
    - B. Anchoring bias.
    - C. Endowment bias.
    - D. Status quo bias.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
      Explanation: Status quo bias is an emotional preference for leaving things as they are, even when a change would clearly be better.
    </details>

16. According to research cited in the textbook, which group is generally found to be more overconfident and risk tolerant when investing?
    - A. Women.
    - B. Men.
    - C. Neither; overconfidence is unrelated to gender.
    - D. Older investors, regardless of gender.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: B
      Explanation: The research cited found that men tend to be more overconfident and risk tolerant, and they trade more as a result.
    </details>

17. The study cited in the textbook found that, compared with male fund managers, female fund managers tended to:
    - A. Trade more often and take on more risk than men.
    - B. Follow less consistent styles and trade more.
    - C. Take less risk, trade less, and have steadier styles.
    - D. Show more overconfidence and higher turnover.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: C
      Explanation: Female managers took less risk, traded less, and followed more consistent investment styles than male managers.
    </details>

18. According to the textbook's summary chart, women tend to be more susceptible to:
    - A. Emotional biases such as endowment and status quo bias.
    - B. Cognitive biases such as overconfidence and availability bias.
    - C. Neither cognitive nor emotional biases.
    - D. Hindsight bias only.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: A
      Explanation: The chart links men more with cognitive biases (overconfidence, availability) and women more with emotional biases (endowment, status quo). Both are susceptible to others, such as loss aversion.
    </details>

19. A representative designs a portfolio that may slightly underperform a theoretically optimal allocation, but that the client can realistically stick with through market ups and downs. This is the client's:
    - A. Risk capacity.
    - B. Strategic asset allocation.
    - C. Efficient portfolio.
    - D. Best practical allocation.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
      Explanation: A best practical allocation adjusts risk and return to the client's behavioural tendencies. It trades a little expected return for a portfolio the client will actually hold on to.
    </details>

20. Under the principles for building a best practical allocation, a very wealthy client with strong behavioural biases should generally have those biases:
    - A. Ignored entirely when building the portfolio.
    - B. Adapted to rather than moderated.
    - C. Moderated as aggressively as possible.
    - D. Treated the same as for a less-wealthy client.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: B
      Explanation: For a wealthy client, a biased allocation is unlikely to threaten their standard of living, so the representative can adapt to the bias rather than fight it.
    </details>

21. Which TWO of the following are principles the textbook uses to decide whether to moderate or adapt to a client's bias? (Choose TWO)
    - A. Biases of clients with less wealth should generally be moderated.
    - B. Biases of clients with less wealth should generally be adapted to.
    - C. Cognitive biases should generally be adapted to.
    - D. All biases should be moderated regardless of wealth.
    - E. Emotional biases should generally be adapted to.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: A, E
      Explanation: Less wealth means a bias could threaten the client's living standard, so moderate it. Emotional biases are hard to correct, so adapt to them. Cognitive biases can usually be corrected with information, so they are moderated.
    </details>

22. In the case study, Michelle tells her advisor to sell her equities and move to money market funds during a downturn, despite a long time horizon and a medium risk profile. Her behaviour most closely illustrates:
    - A. Overconfidence in her own market forecasts.
    - B. A rational response to a shorter time horizon.
    - C. A fear-driven reaction inconsistent with her risk profile.
    - D. Representativeness bias about past bear markets.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: C
      Explanation: Nothing about Michelle's horizon or profile had changed. She was reacting emotionally to the downturn, which is exactly the kind of decision a best practical allocation aims to prevent.
    </details>

23. After markets recovered, Michelle regretted missing the rebound and demanded an entirely aggressive growth portfolio. This later reaction shows that:
    - A. She has become a rational, unbiased investor.
    - B. Her original decision to sell was the correct strategy.
    - C. Her risk profile has permanently shifted to aggressive.
    - D. Emotional biases can swing from one extreme to the other.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
      Explanation: Michelle went from fear to regret-driven greed. The advisor's job is to bring decisions back to her actual goals and risk profile rather than follow her emotions either way.
    </details>

24. In the case study, the advisor lets Pierre keep 5% of his portfolio in a small self-directed account for aggressive trades, with 95% in a properly structured portfolio. This approach illustrates:
    - A. Letting the client's bias override his long-term plan.
    - B. Adapting to his bias while protecting the bulk of the portfolio.
    - C. A breach of the suitability rule for the whole account.
    - D. Moderating his bias through education and information.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: B
      Explanation: The small "play" account satisfies Pierre's urge to trade, while the core portfolio stays aligned with his goals. It's a practical way of adapting to a bias.
    </details>

25. According to the textbook, a key benefit of understanding a client's behavioural biases is that it helps produce:
    - A. A portfolio the client can stick with through up and down markets.
    - B. Above-market returns from exploiting other investors' biases.
    - C. Fewer required reviews of the client's account.
    - D. An exemption from certain suitability obligations.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: A
      Explanation: Understanding a client's biases helps build a portfolio they can stay with through market cycles, which strengthens the advisory relationship. It doesn't change suitability obligations or guarantee returns.
    </details>
