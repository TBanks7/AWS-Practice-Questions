# Chapter 5: Behavioural Finance

Original practice questions grounded in the CSI Investment Funds in Canada (IFC) textbook, Chapter 5: behavioural finance, cognitive vs. emotional biases, gender differences in investing, and building a client's "best practical allocation."

---

1. Behavioural finance is best defined as:
    - A. A branch of economics focused exclusively on interest rate policy.
    - B. A legal framework requiring KYC documentation.
    - C. The application of psychology to understand human behaviour in finance and investing.
    - D. A mathematical model proving that all investors act rationally.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: C
    </details>

2. Standard (traditional) finance theory generally assumes investors are:
    - A. Risk averse and rational in their decision-making.
    - B. Always risk-seeking regardless of circumstances.
    - C. Incapable of any risk assessment.
    - D. Guaranteed to earn above-market returns.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: A
    </details>

3. The tendency for stocks, and small stocks in particular, to move abnormally higher during the month of January is known as:
    - A. The endowment effect.
    - B. Regret aversion.
    - C. Loss aversion.
    - D. The January Effect.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
    </details>

4. A representative notes that one client's bias stems from a subconscious information-processing shortcut, while another client's bias stems from an involuntary emotional reaction. These two biases are best classified, respectively, as:
    - A. Both emotional biases.
    - B. A cognitive bias and an emotional bias.
    - C. Both cognitive biases.
    - D. Neither is a recognized behavioural bias.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: B
    </details>

5. A client refuses to sell a stock for less than the price they originally paid, even though its fundamentals have deteriorated, because they remain fixated on that original purchase price. This illustrates:
    - A. Availability bias.
    - B. Endowment bias.
    - C. Anchoring bias.
    - D. Status quo bias.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: C
    </details>

6. The textbook notes that cognitive biases, unlike emotional biases, can often be corrected through:
    - A. Ignoring the client's concerns entirely.
    - B. Waiting for the market to recover.
    - C. Switching the client to a different representative.
    - D. Better information and advice, since cognitive biases stem from faulty reasoning rather than impulse.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
    </details>

7. An investor receives an unverified stock tip from an acquaintance and immediately invests a large sum, believing they have superior insight compared to other market participants. This illustrates:
    - A. Overconfidence.

    - D. Endowment bias.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>

    </details>

8. A client sees that a new fund shares a few superficial characteristics with a past fund that performed extremely well, and concludes the new fund must also be a great investment, ignoring key differences. This illustrates:

9. After a stock rises sharply, an investor insists they "knew it all along," even though the outcome was genuinely unpredictable at the time. This is an example of:

11. A client demands a much higher price to sell a security they already own than they would be willing to pay to acquire that same security today. This behaviour, inconsistent with standard economic theory, illustrates:
    - A. Endowment bias.

12. In the textbook's diagnostic example, a client is offered a choice between an assured gain of $475 and a 25% chance of gaining $2,000 (with a 75% chance of gaining nothing). Which TWO statements about this scenario are correct? (Choose TWO)
    - A. The rational, expected-value-maximizing choice is the assured $475 gain.
    - B. The rational, expected-value-maximizing choice is the 25% chance at $2,000, since its expected value is $500.
    - C. A loss-averse client is likely to select the probabilistic option.
    - D. A loss-averse client is likely to select the assured $475 gain instead of the higher-expected-value option.
    - E. Loss aversion has no bearing on how a client would respond to this question.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: B, D
    </details>

13. Industry veterans use the term "get-even-itis" to describe a client who:
    - A. Immediately sells any investment at the first sign of a loss.
    - B. Refuses to invest in equities under any circumstances.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: C
    </details>

20. According to the two principles for constructing a best practical allocation, a very wealthy client with strong behavioural biases should generally have those biases:

22. In the case study, Michelle directs her advisor to sell her equity investments and move to money market funds during a downturn, despite having a long-term horizon and a medium risk profile. Her behaviour during the downturn most closely illustrates:
