# Chapter 15: Selecting a Mutual Fund

Original practice questions grounded in the CSI Investment Funds in Canada (IFC) textbook, Chapter 15: sources of mutual fund volatility (unique vs. market risk), the fund selection process, the reward-to-risk ratio, Sharpe ratio, beta and alpha, CIFSC classification, investment philosophies (equity and fixed-income), process and team-based management, style drift, and the Samantha/Christine case study.

---

1. A biotech stock's price can swing sharply on drug-approval news, but that sensitivity largely disappears when the stock is held in a diversified portfolio. The risk being eliminated is:
    - A. Market risk.
    - B. Interest rate risk.
    - C. Unique (specific) risk.
    - D. Exchange rate risk.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: C
      Explanation: Unique risk comes from events affecting one company or industry, and diversification can largely remove it. Market risk affects all securities and can't be diversified away.
    </details>

2. After diversifying to reduce unique risk as far as possible, an investor is still left with:
    - A. Market risk.
    - B. No risk at all.
    - C. Default risk only.
    - D. Unique risk.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: A
      Explanation: Market (systematic) risk comes from economy-wide factors and remains no matter how well diversified the portfolio is.
    </details>

3. A representative wants to help a client reduce default risk in a bond portfolio. The most direct way is to:
    - A. Concentrate in one high-yield issuer.
    - B. Ignore issuers' credit ratings.
    - C. Add foreign currency bonds.
    - D. Avoid concentrating in lower-quality bonds.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
      Explanation: Default risk comes from issuers' credit quality. Avoiding heavy concentration in lower-quality corporate bonds reduces it. Foreign bonds add currency risk instead.
    </details>

4. According to the textbook's risk-return diagram, which sequence orders fund types from LOWEST to HIGHEST risk and expected return?
    - A. Specialty, equity, bond, money market.
    - B. Money market, bond, equity, specialty.
    - C. Bond, money market, specialty, equity.
    - D. Money market, equity, bond, specialty.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: B
      Explanation: Risk and expected return rise from money market funds, to bond funds, to equity funds, to specialty funds, which carry the most concentration risk.
    </details>

5. The reward-to-risk ratio is calculated as:
    - A. Standard deviation ÷ return.
    - B. Beta × alpha.
    - C. Return ÷ standard deviation.
    - D. Risk-free rate ÷ standard deviation.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: C
      Explanation: The reward-to-risk ratio divides return by standard deviation, showing how much return was earned per unit of risk. Unlike the Sharpe ratio, it doesn't subtract the risk-free rate.
    </details>

6. A fund returned 9.8% last year with a standard deviation of 5.5%. What is its reward-to-risk ratio?
    - A. 1.78.
    - B. 0.56.
    - C. 4.3.
    - D. 15.3.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: A
      Explanation: Reward-to-risk = 9.8 / 5.5 ≈ 1.78. Subtracting (9.8 − 5.5 = 4.3) or inverting (5.5 / 9.8 = 0.56) gives the wrong answer.
    </details>

7. A Canadian equity fund returned 6% with a 5% standard deviation; its benchmark returned 8% with a 10% standard deviation. With a 1% risk-free rate, their Sharpe ratios are 1.0 and 0.70. Which conclusion is correct?
    - A. The benchmark did better on a risk-adjusted basis.
    - B. The fund and the benchmark performed identically.
    - C. The benchmark did better because its return was higher.
    - D. The fund did better on a risk-adjusted basis.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
      Explanation: The fund's higher Sharpe ratio means it earned more excess return per unit of risk. The benchmark's higher raw return came with twice the volatility.
    </details>

8. An equity fund that moves up or down more sharply than the overall market has a beta:
    - A. Of exactly 1.0.
    - B. Greater than 1.0.
    - C. Equal to 0.
    - D. That is negative.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: B
      Explanation: A beta above 1.0 means the fund amplifies market moves. A beta of 1.0 moves with the market, and a negative beta moves against it.
    </details>

9. Which TWO of the following correctly describe a fund manager's alpha? (Choose TWO)
    - A. A negative alpha means the manager outperformed for the risk taken.
    - B. Alpha measures the fund's beta relative to the market.
    - C. A positive alpha means the manager added value beyond what beta predicts.
    - D. An alpha of zero means severe underperformance.
    - E. An alpha of zero means normal performance for the risk taken.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: C, E
      Explanation: Alpha is the return above or below what the fund's beta would predict. Positive means value added, zero means the fund earned exactly what its risk implied, and negative means it underperformed for its risk.
    </details>

10. A fund has shown strong performance over 10 years, all under the same manager. According to the textbook, this suggests:
    - A. Skill likely played a role in the results.
    - B. The results were most likely due to luck.
    - C. Manager tenure is irrelevant to past results.
    - D. The fund carries unacceptable key-person risk.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: A
      Explanation: Luck rarely lasts a full market cycle, so strong results sustained over a long period under one manager point to skill.
    </details>

11. The Canadian Investment Funds Standards Committee (CIFSC) is primarily responsible for:
    - A. Setting fund management fees.
    - B. Regulating dealers' sales practices.
    - C. Approving fund prospectuses.
    - D. Classifying funds into standard categories.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
      Explanation: The CIFSC assigns funds to standardized categories based on their actual holdings, which makes peer comparisons meaningful. It doesn't regulate fees, dealers, or prospectuses.
    </details>

12. A manager buys mature, out-of-favour companies for less than their underlying assets are believed to be worth, avoiding paying up for growth. This is:
    - A. Momentum investing.
    - B. Value investing.
    - C. Sector rotation.
    - D. GARP.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: B
      Explanation: Value investors look for stocks priced below their intrinsic value, often with low P/E or price-to-book ratios, and wait for the market to recognize that value.
    </details>

13. A manager focuses on companies' future earnings prospects rather than current price, seeking firms in expanding sectors with strong R&D and returns on capital. This is:
    - A. Value investing.
    - B. Momentum investing.
    - C. Growth investing.
    - D. GARP.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: C
      Explanation: Growth investors pay for strong expected earnings growth and focus less on current valuation. GARP managers also want growth but avoid high P/E ratios.
    </details>

14. A manager believes stocks with strong recent gains in price or earnings will keep rising, often uses quantitative models, and runs a high-turnover portfolio. This is:
    - A. Momentum investing.
    - B. Value investing.
    - C. Sector rotation.
    - D. Growth investing.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: A
      Explanation: Momentum investors bet that recent trends will continue. The approach tends to mean high turnover and higher risk.
    </details>

15. Unlike a pure growth manager, a Growth at a Reasonable Price (GARP) manager:
    - A. Ignores earnings growth entirely.
    - B. Invests only in government bonds.
    - C. Seeks the highest P/E ratios available.
    - D. Seeks earnings growth while avoiding high P/E ratios.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
      Explanation: GARP blends growth and value: it looks for companies with growing earnings and rising ROE but avoids paying excessive P/E multiples.
    </details>

16. A manager overweights industries expected to outperform at the current stage of the economic cycle, focusing more on industry weights than on individual stocks. This is:
    - A. Value investing.
    - B. Sector rotation.
    - C. Momentum investing.
    - D. Security selection.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: B
      Explanation: Sector rotation moves money between industries as the economic cycle unfolds — for example, into cyclicals during an expansion.
    </details>

17. A fixed-income manager shifts between long-term government bonds and short-term T-bills based on a forecast of interest rate movements. This is:
    - A. Sector trading.
    - B. Security selection.
    - C. Interest rate anticipation.
    - D. Sector rotation.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: C
      Explanation: Interest rate anticipation adjusts maturity and duration based on rate forecasts: longer when rates are expected to fall, shorter when they're expected to rise.
    </details>

18. A fixed-income manager evaluates individual bonds using fundamental and credit analysis to judge how likely the promised payments are to be made. This is:
    - A. Security selection.
    - B. Interest rate anticipation.
    - C. Sector trading.
    - D. Momentum investing.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: A
      Explanation: Fixed-income security selection means analyzing each issuer's creditworthiness to find bonds that are mispriced relative to their risk.
    </details>

19. According to the textbook, a team-based investment process, rather than one dominant individual, is advantageous primarily because it:
    - A. Guarantees higher returns.
    - B. Removes the need for risk monitoring.
    - C. Gives one manager unlimited discretion.
    - D. Supports continuity if key people leave.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
      Explanation: A team approach reduces dependence on one person, so the fund's process can continue if a key manager leaves.
    </details>

20. A small-cap fund manager shifts a large part of the portfolio into large-cap stocks while small caps are underperforming. This change in approach over time is called:
    - A. Sector rotation.
    - B. Style drift.
    - C. GARP.
    - D. Tracking error.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: B
      Explanation: Style drift is when a manager moves away from the fund's stated style. It can leave investors holding something other than what they bought.
    </details>

21. Which TWO of the following are methods of style analysis used to detect style drift? (Choose TWO)
    - A. Returns-based style analysis.
    - B. Reviewing the manager's personal portfolio.
    - C. Holdings-based style analysis.
    - D. Reviewing only the Fund Facts document.
    - E. Comparing the fund with the overnight rate.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: A, C
      Explanation: Returns-based analysis compares the fund's returns with style indexes. Holdings-based analysis classifies each holding by style at a point in time.
    </details>

22. In the case study, Christine decided the XYZ US Equity Fund was not suitable for Samantha, a conservative investor, primarily because the fund:
    - A. Had a low beta and low volatility.
    - B. Was fully hedged against currency risk.
    - C. Held only dividend-paying large caps.
    - D. Had a high beta and was unhedged.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
      Explanation: The XYZ fund held mid-caps, had a beta of 1.2, a standard deviation of about ±19%, and no currency hedging — too much risk for a conservative client.
    </details>

23. Christine recommended the LMN US Dividend Fund instead, largely because it had:
    - A. A beta of 1.2 and no currency hedging.
    - B. A lower beta and volatility, and full hedging.
    - C. The highest return regardless of risk.
    - D. No performance history available.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: B
      Explanation: The LMN fund had a beta of 0.7, lower volatility, full currency hedging, and a long record of steady returns, which suited Samantha's conservative profile.
    </details>

24. In the case study, the LMN US Dividend Fund's 5-star Morningstar rating reflects:
    - A. The fund's asset size.
    - B. Its sales commission structure.
    - C. Its risk-adjusted performance versus peers.
    - D. Guaranteed future outperformance.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: C
      Explanation: Star ratings rank past risk-adjusted performance against similar funds. They say nothing about future results.
    </details>

25. For category assignment, the CIFSC groups securities into five broad asset classes. They are:
    - A. Cash, fixed income, equity, commodity, and other.
    - B. Growth, value, momentum, GARP, and sector.
    - C. Canadian, U.S., international, global, and emerging.
    - D. Money market, mortgage, bond, balanced, and specialty.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: A
      Explanation: The CIFSC's five broad asset classes are cash, fixed income, equity, commodity, and other. Fund categories are then built on those classes.
    </details>

26. The textbook notes that even after a rigorous selection process, there's no guarantee a fund will keep performing as expected, because investors are, in a sense:
    - A. Guaranteed accurate forecasts from any process.
    - B. Free of risk once a fund is chosen.
    - C. Protected from manager turnover.
    - D. "Prisoners of past data."

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
      Explanation: Fund selection has to rely on historical data, and the future won't necessarily look like the past. Ongoing monitoring is still needed.
    </details>

27. According to the textbook, a fund that grows very large relative to its category may face a disadvantage because:
    - A. It qualifies for lower management fees.
    - B. Large trades can move prices in a small market.
    - C. It is prohibited from holding cash.
    - D. It is exempt from Fund Facts rules.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: B
      Explanation: In Canada's relatively small market, a very large fund's buying and selling can move prices, which makes it harder to change positions quickly.
    </details>

28. An equity fund with less than three years of performance history is a concern primarily because:
    - A. Regulators prohibit recommending new funds.
    - B. Short histories always overstate risk.
    - C. It doesn't show performance over a full market cycle.
    - D. New funds can't publish a Fund Facts document.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: C
      Explanation: A short track record doesn't show how the fund and its manager handle different market conditions, so it's a weak basis for judging future performance.
    </details>
