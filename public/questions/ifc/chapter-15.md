# Chapter 15: Selecting a Mutual Fund

Original practice questions grounded in the CSI Investment Funds in Canada (IFC) textbook, Chapter 15: sources of mutual fund volatility (unique vs. market risk), the fund selection process, the reward-to-risk ratio, Sharpe ratio, beta and alpha, CIFSC classification, investment philosophies (equity and fixed-income), process and team-based management, style drift, and the Samantha/Christine case study.

---

1. A representative explains to a client that a bio-tech stock's price might swing sharply on drug-approval news, but that this specific sensitivity largely disappears once the stock is held within a diversified portfolio. The risk being eliminated through diversification is:
    - A. Market risk.
    - B. Interest rate risk.
    - C. Unique (specific) risk.
    - D. Exchange rate risk.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: C
    </details>

2. After diversifying a portfolio to reduce unique risk as much as possible, an investor will still be left with:
    - A. Market risk, which cannot be eliminated through diversification.
    - B. No risk whatsoever.
    - C. Only default risk.
    - D. Only exchange rate risk.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: A
    </details>

7. A Canadian equity fund earned a 6% return with a 5% standard deviation, while its benchmark earned 8% with a 10% standard deviation. The risk-free rate averaged 1%. Comparing Sharpe ratios (fund: 1.0; benchmark: 0.70), which conclusion is correct?
    - A. The benchmark outperformed the fund on a risk-adjusted basis.
    - B. The fund and benchmark performed identically.
    - C. The comparison is meaningless since the benchmark had a higher total return.
    - D. The fund earned a better risk-adjusted return than the benchmark, despite the benchmark's higher raw return, since the benchmark took on more risk to achieve it.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
    </details>

8. An equity fund that moves up or down more sharply than the overall stock market would have a beta:
    - A. Equal to exactly 1.0.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>

9. Which TWO of the following correctly describe a fund manager's alpha? (Choose TWO)
    - A. A negative alpha indicates the manager outperformed for the level of risk taken.
    - B. Alpha measures the fund's beta relative to the market.
    - C. A positive alpha indicates the manager produced more return than predicted by the fund's beta, adding value.
    - D. An alpha of zero indicates severe underperformance.
    - E. An alpha of zero indicates the manager achieved only normal performance for the risk taken, adding no extra value.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: C, E
    </details>

10. A fund has shown strong performance over 10 years, all under the same manager. What does this suggest, according to the textbook?
    - A. The continuation of good performance is likely partly attributable to the manager's skill, since good luck is less likely to persist over a full market cycle.
    - B. The performance must be entirely due to luck, since a single manager cannot add value.
