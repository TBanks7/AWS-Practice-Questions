# Chapter 16: Mutual Fund Fees and Services

Original practice questions grounded in the CSI Investment Funds in Canada (IFC) textbook, Chapter 16: sales charges and management fees, explicit and implicit costs (MER/TER), accumulation plans and dollar cost averaging, the five types of systematic withdrawal plans, and the taxation of mutual fund distributions and redemptions.

---

1. Sales charges (loads) are charged to investors when they buy or sell units. Management fees, by contrast, are:
    - A. Charged only on redemptions in the first year.
    - B. Paid by the investor directly, outside the fund.
    - C. Charged only when units are purchased.
    - D. Paid by the fund and charged against its earnings.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
      Explanation: Management fees are expenses of the fund itself, deducted from its assets and earnings. Investors pay them indirectly through lower returns.
    </details>

2. A sales charge paid when units are purchased is a front-end load. A sales charge paid when units are redeemed is a:
    - A. Trustee fee.
    - B. Back-end load.
    - C. Trailer fee.
    - D. Set-up fee.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: B
      Explanation: A back-end load, or deferred sales charge (DSC), is paid on redemption and usually declines the longer the units are held.
    </details>

3. An ongoing annual fee, based on a percentage of assets, paid to a representative for servicing an existing client, is called a:
    - A. Trailer fee.
    - B. Front-end load.
    - C. Redemption fee.
    - D. Switch fee.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: A
      Explanation: Trailer fees (trailing commissions) are paid out of the management fee for as long as the client holds the fund, as compensation for ongoing service and advice.
    </details>

4. A client invests $1,000 in a fund with a 4% front-end load. How much is actually invested in fund units?
    - A. $1,000.
    - B. $1,040.
    - C. $960.
    - D. $600.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: C
      Explanation: The 4% load ($40) is deducted from the $1,000, so $960 goes into the fund.
    </details>

5. A fund's NAVPS is $12, and it charges a 4% front-end load. What is the offering (purchase) price per unit?
    - A. $12.00.
    - B. $11.52.
    - C. $12.48.
    - D. $12.50.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
      Explanation: Offering price = NAVPS / (1 − load) = $12 / 0.96 = $12.50. The load is a percentage of the total amount paid, so simply adding 4% to NAVPS ($12.48) is wrong.
    </details>

6. Under a typical deferred sales charge schedule, the redemption fee:
    - A. Increases the longer units are held.
    - B. Declines the longer units are held, eventually to zero.
    - C. Stays fixed regardless of the holding period.
    - D. Applies only in the first month.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: B
      Explanation: DSC schedules typically start at around 5–6% and step down each year, reaching zero after about six or seven years.
    </details>

7. An investor bought units at a NAVPS of $10 and redeems in year four, when NAVPS is $15 and the DSC is 3%. If the charge is based on the ORIGINAL purchase price, the redemption proceeds per unit are:
    - A. $14.70.
    - B. $14.55.
    - C. $15.00.
    - D. $10.30.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: A
      Explanation: DSC = 3% × $10 = $0.30, so proceeds = $15.00 − $0.30 = $14.70. If the DSC were based on the current value, it would be $0.45, leaving $14.55.
    </details>

8. A fee sometimes charged when an investor switches from one fund to another managed by the same company is a:
    - A. Trustee fee.
    - B. Set-up fee.
    - C. Transfer fee.
    - D. Closing fee.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: C
      Explanation: Transfer (switch) fees may apply when moving between funds in the same family. In a non-registered account, the switch may also trigger a taxable disposition.
    </details>

9. Annual fees charged for holding mutual funds within an RRSP, RRIF, or RESP are known as:
    - A. Front-end loads.
    - B. Redemption fees.
    - C. Short-term trading fees.
    - D. Trustee fees.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
      Explanation: Registered plans need a trustee, and the annual trustee fee is charged for administering the plan itself, separately from fund-level expenses.
    </details>

10. The management expense ratio (MER) represents:
    - A. Sales charges as a percentage of each purchase.
    - B. Total annual fund expenses as a percentage of assets.
    - C. Trading commissions as a percentage of fund assets.
    - D. A one-time charge as a percentage of the initial deposit.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: B
      Explanation: MER = management fees plus operating expenses (and taxes) divided by average net assets. Trading commissions are excluded and reported separately as the TER.
    </details>

11. Which TWO of the following affect the size of a fund's MER? (Choose TWO)
    - A. The type of assets the fund invests in.
    - B. The client's marginal tax rate.
    - C. The size of the fund.
    - D. The client's province of residence.
    - E. The number of units the client owns.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: A, C
      Explanation: Complex mandates (such as foreign equities) cost more to manage than simple ones (such as T-bills), and smaller funds spread fixed costs over fewer assets. Investor-specific factors don't change a fund's MER.
    </details>

12. A fund's MER is 2.42% and its trading expense ratio (TER) is 0.07%. What are the fund's total expenses as shown in Fund Facts?
    - A. 2.42%.
    - B. 0.07%.
    - C. 2.35%.
    - D. 2.49%.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
      Explanation: Fund Facts shows total fund expenses as MER + TER = 2.42% + 0.07% = 2.49%.
    </details>

13. A fund's turnover rate measures:
    - A. The fund's total assets under management.
    - B. The share of the portfolio bought and sold in a year.
    - C. The number of unitholders who redeemed.
    - D. The fund's annual dividend yield.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: B
      Explanation: Turnover shows how actively the portfolio is traded. Higher turnover generally means higher trading costs and potentially more taxable distributions.
    </details>

14. Brokerage commissions a fund pays when buying and selling securities are:
    - A. Implicit costs disclosed as the TER.
    - B. Explicit costs included in the MER.
    - C. Costs that can't be charged to a fund.
    - D. Costs with no effect on returns.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: A
      Explanation: Trading costs aren't in the MER, but they still reduce returns. They're reported separately as the trading expense ratio.
    </details>

15. According to the textbook, fund costs have a greater relative impact on returns for:
    - A. Aggressive small-cap equity funds.
    - B. Specialty sector funds.
    - C. Fixed-income and index funds.
    - D. All funds equally.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: C
      Explanation: Funds with lower expected returns, such as fixed-income and index funds, lose a bigger share of their return to the same level of fees.
    </details>

16. A facility that lets an investor make automatic periodic purchases of a mutual fund is a(n):
    - A. Systematic withdrawal plan.
    - B. Ratio withdrawal plan.
    - C. Fixed-period plan.
    - D. Accumulation plan.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
      Explanation: An accumulation (pre-authorized investment) plan invests a set amount automatically at regular intervals. Withdrawal plans do the reverse.
    </details>

17. The key benefit of dollar cost averaging, as shown by investing a fixed amount monthly instead of in two lump sums, is that it:
    - A. Guarantees higher returns than lump sums.
    - B. Tends to lower the average cost per unit.
    - C. Eliminates all investment risk.
    - D. Requires a larger minimum investment.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: B
      Explanation: A fixed dollar amount buys more units when prices are low and fewer when they're high, which tends to lower the average cost per unit and removes the need to time the market.
    </details>

18. In a steadily falling market, dollar cost averaging will generally result in:
    - A. A higher average cost than buying at the bottom.
    - B. A guaranteed loss for the investor.
    - C. No units being purchased.
    - D. The lowest possible average cost.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: A
      Explanation: Buying at the very bottom is always cheapest in hindsight. Dollar cost averaging won't match that, but it avoids investing everything at the wrong time.
    </details>

19. Under a fixed-dollar (constant) withdrawal plan, the client:
    - A. Receives a fixed % of current value.
    - B. Receives the balance divided by years remaining.
    - C. Receives a fixed dollar amount each period.
    - D. Receives payments for life, based on mortality tables.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: C
      Explanation: A fixed-dollar plan pays the same amount each period. If withdrawals are more than the fund earns, capital is eroded. Option A is a ratio plan, B a fixed-period plan, and D a life plan.
    </details>

20. Under a ratio withdrawal plan, because the percentage is always applied to the current portfolio value:
    - A. The investment will be exhausted within five years.
    - B. The withdrawal amount never changes.
    - C. The plan is identical to a life plan.
    - D. The investment is technically never fully exhausted.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
      Explanation: Taking a percentage of whatever remains never reaches zero (unless the ratio is 100%), but the dollar amount paid out changes as the fund's value rises and falls.
    </details>

21. Under a fixed-period withdrawal plan, each year's withdrawal is calculated as:
    - A. A fixed dollar amount set at the start.
    - B. The remaining value divided by years remaining.
    - C. A fixed % of the original investment.
    - D. An amount based solely on life expectancy.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: B
      Explanation: A fixed-period plan divides the current value by the number of years left, so the fund is used up exactly at the end of the chosen period.
    </details>

22. Which TWO statements about life withdrawal plans and annuities are true? (Choose TWO)
    - A. A life plan uses the client's life expectancy as the period.
    - B. An annuity is offered by the fund itself, not an insurer.
    - C. A variable annuity pays a fixed amount regardless of performance.
    - D. An annuity is a contract with a life insurance company.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: A, D
      Explanation: A life plan works like a fixed-period plan, using the client's expected remaining lifetime from mortality tables. An annuity is a contract with a life insurer that pays the annuitant regular income.
    </details>

23. Unlike most annuities, which pay a fixed amount, a variable annuity's payments:
    - A. Never change once the contract begins.
    - B. Are guaranteed regardless of performance.
    - C. Fluctuate with the underlying fund.
    - D. Increase every year.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: C
      Explanation: Variable annuity payments rise and fall with the value of the mutual fund they are paid from.
    </details>

24. A fund distributes $6 per unit in capital gains at year-end, and its NAVPS falls from $30 to $24. An investor who bought units on December 1, just before the distribution, will:
    - A. Avoid tax, having held units less than a month.
    - B. Owe tax on the full $6 distribution.
    - C. Receive a refund of the distribution.
    - D. See no change in the NAVPS.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: B
      Explanation: Whoever holds units on the distribution date is taxed on the full distribution, even though the gains built up before they bought. This is sometimes called "buying a tax liability."
    </details>

25. Outside a registered plan, a unitholder of a mutual fund trust receives a T3 slip, while a shareholder of a mutual fund corporation receives a:
    - A. T5 slip.
    - B. T4 slip.
    - C. T4A slip.
    - D. T4RSP slip.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: A
      Explanation: Trusts report income on T3 slips, and corporations report dividends on T5 slips. T4 and T4A slips report employment and pension income, and T4RSP slips report RRSP withdrawals.
    </details>

26. An investor bought fund units at $11 and sold them at $16, a $5 capital gain per share. How much must be reported as taxable income per share?
    - A. $5.00.
    - B. $1.25.
    - C. $3.75.
    - D. $2.50.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
      Explanation: Taxable capital gain = 50% × $5.00 = $2.50 per share.
    </details>

27. When a fund manager sells a security within the portfolio at a loss, the capital loss:
    - A. Passes through to unitholders as a deduction.
    - B. Eliminates the fund's tax reporting.
    - C. Stays in the fund to offset future gains.
    - D. Is converted into an interest expense.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: C
      Explanation: Gains flow out to unitholders, but losses don't. The fund keeps them and uses them to offset capital gains it realizes later.
    </details>

28. An investor who reinvests all distributions must track their adjusted cost base (ACB) primarily to avoid:
    - A. Paying any tax at all.
    - B. Being taxed twice on the same income.
    - C. Losing the right of redemption.
    - D. Triggering short-term trading fees.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: B
      Explanation: Reinvested distributions were already taxed when paid, so they're added to the ACB. Otherwise they'd be taxed again as part of the gain when the units are sold.
    </details>

29. An investor invested $10,000 and reinvested $3,500 of distributions (already taxed) over the years. The holding is later sold for $18,000. What is the capital gain?
    - A. $4,500.
    - B. $8,000.
    - C. $2,250.
    - D. $3,500.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: A
      Explanation: ACB = $10,000 + $3,500 = $13,500, so the capital gain is $18,000 − $13,500 = $4,500 (half of that, $2,250, is taxable). Ignoring the reinvested distributions would overstate the gain at $8,000.
    </details>

30. When a fund distribution is automatically reinvested rather than paid in cash, the investor ends up with:
    - A. Fewer units, each worth more.
    - B. The same units at the same NAVPS.
    - C. A tax-free increase in value.
    - D. More units, each worth less.

    <details markdown=1><summary markdown='span'>Answer</summary>
      Correct answer: D
      Explanation: NAVPS drops by the amount of the distribution, and the reinvested amount buys additional units. The total value is the same right after the distribution, but it's still taxable.
    </details>
